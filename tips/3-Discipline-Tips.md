## PART 3: MOTIVATION & DISCIPLINE PRINCIPLES (25)

### Staying in the Game

> ***81. Treat hunting like a marathon, not a sprint*** - You cannot sprint for years. Pacing matters. Some days you review for four hours. Some days you review for 30 minutes. Consistency over intensity wins the long game.

> ***82. Build a daily minimum viable effort*** - On your worst days, do 15 minutes of review. That's it. Just show up. The habit is more important than the output. Fifteen minutes keeps the streak alive.

> ***83. Reward the process, not the outcome*** - Did you follow your methodology? Did you document your findings? Did you rest properly? Reward these. Outcome (finding a bug) is partially luck. Process is fully within your control.

> ***84. Keep a "done list" alongside a "to-do list"*** - You did something today. Maybe you read a deploy script. Maybe you mapped storage. Write it down. Seeing progress, even small progress, builds momentum.

> ***85. Schedule your hardest work for your best hours*** - Are you a morning person? Review complex systems then. Afternoon slump? Do documentation, tooling, or learning. Match difficulty to energy. Don't fight your own biology.

---

### Handling Competition

> ***86. Assume others are looking where you're looking*** - If a protocol is trending, dozens of hunters are already reviewing it. The easy bugs are gone. The remaining bugs require deeper understanding or novel angles. Don't compete on speed; compete on depth.

> ***87. Turn jealousy into curiosity*** - See someone else's big finding? Instead of "why not me?" ask "how did they think of that?" Study their approach. Jealousy is wasted energy. Curiosity compounds.

> ***88. Build relationships with researchers ahead of you*** - Ask specific questions. Offer to review their draft reports. Show respect for their time. Most top researchers are generous if you approach with genuine humility.

> ***89. Ignore Twitter brags*** - Social media shows highlights, not the months of dry spells. Every big find is preceded by hundreds of hours of silence. Don't let curated success distort your perception.

> ***90. Compete only with yesterday's you*** - Did you understand something today that you didn't understand yesterday? That's winning. Did you document something you would have forgotten? That's winning. Leaderboards are noise.

---

### Professional Habits

> ***91. Show up before you feel ready*** - You don't need to feel confident to start. Action precedes motivation. Sit down, open the code, begin. The feeling follows the action.

> ***92. Stop before you want to stop*** - End each session while you still have curiosity left. That residual interest pulls you back tomorrow. Draining yourself completely creates resistance.

> ***93. Protect your mornings for deep work*** - Before email, before Slack, before Twitter — review. The first 90 minutes of your day are your highest-leverage hours. Don't give them to notifications.

> ***94. Do the hard thing first*** - The function you're dreading to review? Do it now. The system you don't understand? Start there. Procrastination drains more energy than the hard work itself.

> ***95. Create external accountability*** - Tell a friend you'll review a protocol by Friday. Join a study group with weekly check-ins. Publish a timeline for your next write-up. External pressure beats internal willpower.

---

### Learning Discipline

> ***96. Study one new thing every week*** - One new EIP. One new primitive. One new attack vector from an old report. Small weekly investments compound into deep expertise over years.

> ***97. Review your own old reports quarterly*** - Read what you wrote six months ago. You'll be embarrassed by what you missed. That's not shame — that's proof of growth.

> ***98. Re-implement primitives from scratch*** - Don't just read ERC-4626. Write your own. You don't truly understand a standard until you've built it and broken it yourself.

> ***99. Teach a concept to an imaginary beginner*** - If you can't explain flash loan mechanics to someone who knows nothing, you don't understand it well enough. Teaching reveals gaps.

> ***100. Keep a "lessons learned" file*** - Every time you miss a bug in a contest or see a novel finding, write one sentence about what you learned. Review this file monthly. Patterns emerge.
